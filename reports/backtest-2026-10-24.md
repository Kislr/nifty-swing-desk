## How to read this

Expectancy is the average R (multiple of the initial risk) per trade after costs; above +0.2R with a profit factor above 1.3 over 100+ trades is a system worth trading small. Max DD is the deepest peak-to-trough fall in cumulative R: with 1% risk per trade, 10R of drawdown ≈ 10% of capital. The P&L column uses fixed-fraction sizing at the configured capital with no compounding. Fewer than ~60 trades in a slice is noise.

Known simplifications: fills assume the stop order is working at the open; no partial exits; survivorship bias if the universe list is today's constituents (names that left the index are absent); corporate actions (splits, bonuses) are not adjusted in bhavcopy data and can produce false stop-outs on ex-dates.

## What this says

1. **The edge, if any, is small and inside the noise.** With ~150 trades and a per-trade standard deviation near 1R, the standard error of expectancy is about ±0.08R. Every line above sits within one or two of those of zero. Two years of history cannot rank these configurations; a longer backfill (`node scripts/backfill.mjs --years 5`) is the single most valuable next step.
2. **The 2.5R target was effectively never reached** (8 of 143 exits), so the old exit rule was really a 15-day time stop. 2R is adopted as the default because targets are now hit in a meaningful share of trades and each setup alone is positive at 2R — not because the combined number improved; it did not.
3. **Each setup alone is mildly positive at 2R; together they are slightly negative.** The two compete for the same four slots and the one-per-sector rule, and the trades that get crowded out are the better ones. Worth testing: separate slot budgets per setup, or dropping the sector rule for breakouts.
4. **The score does not separate winners from losers.** In every run the 70+ band had the worst expectancy and 60–69 the best (n = 13–17, so this is a hint, not a result). The likely culprit is that +15 for a leading sector and +10 for volume reward exactly the crowded, extended names the breakout rule then loses on. Until the bands separate, treat the score as a sort order only.
5. **About half of all exits are time stops.** Trades mostly go nowhere for 15 days. A trailing exit (close below the 10- or 20-day average) is the obvious replacement to test, one change at a time.
